# Software Development Life Cycle Models

The software development life cycle (SDLC) is a structured process that describes the stages a software product goes through from initial idea to retirement. The typical stages are requirements analysis, design, implementation, testing, deployment and maintenance. Different SDLC models arrange these stages in different ways, and choosing a suitable model is one of the first decisions a project team makes.

## The waterfall model

The waterfall model is a linear sequential model in which each phase must be completed before the next phase begins. Requirements are gathered and signed off at the start, then the system is designed, built, tested and deployed in order. Waterfall works well when requirements are stable and well understood, such as in safety-critical or contract-driven projects. Its main weakness is that customers see working software very late, so misunderstood requirements are expensive to fix.

## The V-model

The V-model extends the waterfall model by pairing every development phase with a corresponding testing phase. Requirements are linked to acceptance testing, system design to system testing, and detailed design to unit testing. Planning the tests early improves quality, but the V-model shares the inflexibility of the waterfall approach.

## Iterative and incremental development

Iterative development builds the system through repeated cycles, refining the product each time based on feedback. Incremental development delivers the system in small usable pieces, adding functionality with each increment. Combining the two reduces risk because problems are discovered early and stakeholders can use parts of the system long before the project ends.

## The spiral model

The spiral model, introduced by Barry Boehm, is a risk-driven model that combines iterative development with systematic risk analysis. Each loop of the spiral has four activities: determine objectives, identify and resolve risks, develop and test, and plan the next iteration. The spiral model is suited to large, expensive and high-risk projects, but it requires experienced staff who can assess risk accurately.

## Agile models

Agile development is an iterative approach that values working software, customer collaboration and responding to change. Frameworks such as Scrum and Extreme Programming deliver software in short iterations of one to four weeks. Agile teams welcome changing requirements, even late in development, which makes the approach popular for products in fast-moving markets.

## Choosing a model

The right model depends on the clarity of the requirements, the size and risk of the project, the experience of the team and the involvement of the customer. Stable, regulated projects often favour waterfall or the V-model, while uncertain and evolving products benefit from iterative, incremental or agile approaches. Many organisations use hybrid models that combine upfront planning with agile delivery.
