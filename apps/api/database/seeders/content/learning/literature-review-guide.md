# Writing a Literature Review

A literature review is a critical summary of the published research on a topic. It shows what is already known, identifies debates and gaps, and explains how a new study will contribute to the field. In a research report or dissertation, the literature review justifies the research question and the chosen methodology.

## Searching for sources

Start with a clear research question and a list of search terms, including synonyms. Use academic databases such as IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, Scopus and Google Scholar rather than general web search. Boolean operators such as AND, OR and NOT combine terms to narrow or broaden a search. Keep a search log that records the databases, search strings and number of results, because it makes the review reproducible.

## Evaluating sources

Peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers are the most reliable academic sources. Evaluate each source for relevance, credibility, currency and methodological quality. Check who the authors are, where the work was published and how often it has been cited. Be cautious with preprints and grey literature, and never rely on a single study for an important claim.

## Organising the review

A literature review should be organised around themes, concepts or debates rather than as a list of individual papers. A thematic structure groups studies that address the same issue and compares their findings. A synthesis matrix is a table that records each source against the key themes, which makes patterns and contradictions easy to see. Chronological organisation is useful only when the development of ideas over time is itself important.

## Critical analysis

Critical analysis means evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of studies instead of simply describing them. Compare research methods, sample sizes and contexts, and explain why studies reach different conclusions. Identify the research gap, which is the question that existing studies have not answered convincingly. The gap should lead directly to the aims of your own project.

## Referencing and academic integrity

Every idea taken from another author must be cited, whether it is quoted, paraphrased or summarised. Use the referencing style required by your department, such as Harvard or IEEE, consistently throughout the report. Reference management tools such as Zotero or Mendeley store sources and generate citations automatically. Plagiarism, including copying text without quotation marks, is a serious academic offence.

## Common mistakes

Common mistakes include summarising one paper per paragraph without synthesis, relying on outdated sources, ignoring studies that contradict your argument and failing to link the review to the research question. Leave enough time for revision, because a strong literature review usually goes through several drafts.
